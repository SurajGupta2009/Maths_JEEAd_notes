# Scope — Chapter 19: Applications of Derivatives

**Official JEE Advanced 2026 anchor (Differential Calculus):** derivatives as rates of change; geometrical interpretation through tangents and normals; increasing and decreasing functions; and maxima and minima of functions of one variable. This chapter applies the derivative machinery developed in Chapter 18 and uses the Mean Value Theorem to justify monotonicity.

Official syllabus PDF: <https://jeeadv.ac.in/documents/jee-advanced-2026-syllabus.pdf>.

## Part I — JEE core and close applications

- Average and instantaneous rates, related rates for circles, cubes, balloons and the 13 m ladder, with units and signs retained.
- Tangents, normals, parallel chords, angles between curves, orthogonal intersections, tangent/normal segment lengths, subtangents and subnormals.
- Differentials and linear approximation for $\sqrt{25.4}$, $(1.01)^{10}$ and $\ln(1.02)$, including direct values and error signs.
- Monotonicity through the derivative sign, strict increase with isolated stationary points, logarithmic inequalities and root counting on monotone branches.
- First- and second-derivative tests, local and absolute extrema, closed-interval candidate comparison and the global minimum of $x+1/x$.
- Convexity, concavity, inflection through a sign change, supporting lines and complete curve-sketching features.
- Feasible optimisation with endpoints: fencing, an open box, fixed-sum products, fixed-perimeter rectangles and a three-factor product.
- Rectangle-in-ellipse optimisation and the corrected rectangle-with-semicircle window model, including its physical feasibility interval.
- Six visual-method sheets joining exact calculations to a physical ladder, tangent angles, magnified linearisation error, cubic roots, endpoint extrema and the window area curve.

## Part II — Olympiad / specialised extensions

- General supporting-line Jensen, the exponential Jensen inequality and the global bound $\cos x\geq1-x^2/2$.
- Young's inequality, two- and three-variable AM–GM, the signed square $a^2+b^2\geq2ab$, and Cauchy–Schwarz in Engel form.
- Envelopes of line families, contact with $y^2=4ax$, and perpendicular parabola tangents meeting on the directrix.
- The ellipse director circle, its circle special case, and Lagrange multipliers with an independent global completed-square proof.
- Symmetric optimisation through $t=x+y$ and $s=xy$, including equal-variable minima, a positive-plane product, and the open-boundary cubic counterexample.

These are explicitly labelled topic-linked extensions, not a complete IOQM/RMO course. The basic source questions on supporting lines and positive constrained products also appear in Part I; their more general inequality and symmetry methods remain in Part II. No extension is blended into the core without a label.

## Chapter boundaries

- **Chapter 18:** derivative definitions, differentiation rules, implicit/parametric methods and higher derivatives remain with Differentiability & Methods. This chapter uses those results rather than re-deriving the full computational catalogue.
- **Chapter 20 onward:** antiderivatives, definite integrals, areas and differential-equation solution methods are not introduced. A rate equation is differentiated here only to solve the stated application.
- **General inequality and geometry courses:** Jensen, Young, envelopes and director circles are included only in the derivative-linked forms present in the source; they are not a complete inequalities or analytic-geometry syllabus.

## Conventions and hypotheses

- In related rates, all geometric quantities remain variable until after differentiation with respect to time. A negative rate is interpreted as decrease.
- A finite-slope normal formula requires $f'(a)\ne0$; horizontal and vertical tangent cases are handled separately. Tangent-length formulas to the $x$-axis use the stated nonzero, nondegenerate cases.
- Positive derivative is sufficient, not necessary, for strict increase. The function $x^3$ remains strictly increasing although its derivative vanishes at $0$.
- An interior stationary point is only a candidate. Local classification uses a sign change or a valid second-derivative test; absolute extrema also require endpoints and nondifferentiable points where applicable.
- An inflection point requires a concavity change, not merely $f''(c)=0$. On closed bounded intervals, continuous functions attain extrema; on open or unbounded domains, a supremum need not be attained.
- Feasible optimisation retains the physical domain after eliminating a variable. Degenerate endpoints are evaluated using the actual model, not automatically assigned zero area.
- Supporting-line Jensen requires convexity and weights summing to one. Lagrange multipliers produce constrained candidates; global attainment is proved separately.
- For positive variables with fixed sum $t$, $0<s=xy\leq t^2/4$. Symmetry alone does not decide whether equal variables give a maximum or minimum; monotonicity in $s$ does.
